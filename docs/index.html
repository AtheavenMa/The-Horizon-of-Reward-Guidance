Certifying robust progress

At a fixed policy state, the available preference labels restrict a true preference parameter δ to an uncertainty set. The scalar example uses δ ∈ [−1,1]. The deployed guidance parameter is φ, whereas vt is the moving target associated with the policy state.

The normalized true progress is jδ(t) = g + δ(φ(t) − vt). The minimum over the compatible preferences is L(t) = g − η0|φ(t) − vt|.

For the example at t = 5, g = 1, η0 = 1, v = 0.12 and φ = 0.2. Thus jδ(5) = 1 − 0.4δ, and the worst compatible value is 0.6. The interactive slider changes the unknown true preference, not the feedback dataset.

Pointwise availability versus an admissible reward path

The certifying reward interval at time t is [vt − (g − γ)/η0, vt + (g − γ)/η0], with g = 1, γ = 0.6, η0 = 1, and v = 0.12. The pointwise choice φ = vt always gives certified gain g = 1.

All admissible continuous reward trajectories start at φ(0) = 0 and satisfy |φ̇(t)| ≤ ρ = 0.04. The furthest they can move in the positive direction by time t is ρt. At t = 5 this fastest path reaches the lower boundary; at t > 5 no admissible path can keep the certificate. The moving interval itself never disappears.

This is a model of certified improvement, not a prediction that every actually trained policy must get worse after time five.

The exact support horizon theorem

The model sets jδ(t) = g + δ[φ(t) − vt], δ ∈ [−η0,η0], φ(0) = 0 and |φ̇| ≤ ρ. The required minimum improvement is γ with 0 < γ < g, η0 > 0 and 0 ≤ ρ < v.

The worst-compatible guarantee is L(t) = g − η0|φ(t) − vt|. Every admissible path has φ(t) ≤ ρt, and φ(t) = ρt attains the largest guarantee when v > ρ. Thus T0 = (g − γ)/[η0(v − ρ)] is both necessary and sufficient for the support duration.

Equality is certified at t = T0; failure occurs strictly afterward. The formula assumes nonbinding amplitude constraints. When ρ ≥ v, speed alone does not impose a finite horizon under this assumption.

What preference feedback can establish

The theorem fixes a target time T beyond the source support horizon. For the example, Δ = g − γ = 0.4, aT = (v − ρ)T = 1.2, and the dynamic feasibility boundary is δc = Δ/aT = 1/3. The instance distance is dT = δc − δ*.

Under the bounded adaptive Bradley–Terry binary-comparison assumptions and appropriate informativeness conditions, expected necessary label count is of order dT−2 log(1/α); a matching-order fixed-batch sufficient bound is also derived.

The values shown for δ* = −0.2, 0, and +0.2 are lower bounds of 111.797, 286.199 and 1788.747 comparisons, respectively. More data can narrow uncertainty and extend a certificate, but not all added labels are equally informative. These are not measurements of final trained policy return.

What the experiments do and do not establish

Deep-Sea-Treasure: after 160 policy updates averaged over eight training runs, original preference information guidance had mean true return 0.683 and deeper-treasure probability 45.6%. Ordinary policy gradient using the true reward yielded return 0.638 and deeper-treasure probability 0.8%. The controllers also differ in how guidance is selected. This experiment identifies trajectory differences, not a universal superiority of incomplete reward knowledge.

Human TL;DR feedback: Qwen2.5-0.5B reward-model accuracy rose from 50.5% with 257 pairs to 56.3% with 4,098 pairs, but a length-and-overlap logistic baseline reached 58.3% on the independent final holdout.

Pythia-1B: a policy was updated for 64 steps with a fixed-reward comparison arm. The changing-reward arm added 96 AI-labeled comparisons in three batches and obeyed the per-event score-movement limit of 0.05. Its final held-out ROUGE-L difference was −0.001722, paired 95% interval [−0.015887, +0.012443], across two training seeds. No confirmed gain was observed.

Takeaways

A reward that works now
may not be a reward we can keep following.

Availability is not reachability
Pointwise certifying rewards may exist even when no bounded-speed reward path can sustain them.
A guarantee has a horizon
The scalar model gives its exact duration; ordinary policy gradient exhibits the same separation locally.
Feedback has an instance-dependent value
Labels can extend certification, but their required number depends on distance to dynamic infeasibility.
The question is not just which reward fits the data. Can it keep up?

Where the claims stop

The exact support-horizon formula is proved for the stated scalar model, including the reward-speed and uncertainty assumptions. A local ordinary Euclidean policy-gradient construction also has pointwise feasibility but fails trajectory certification under finite adaptation.

The Bradley–Terry comparison bounds measure information needed to certify an instance-specific longer horizon. Finite RL and language-model results examine policy behavior and reward-model updates, but do not provide a formal support-horizon theorem for the neural network.

The interactive scalar graphics use the theorem's illustrative parameters. The Deep-Sea-Treasure and Pythia panels report recorded experimental measurements, not simulated curves.
